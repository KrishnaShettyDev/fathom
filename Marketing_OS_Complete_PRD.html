PART 1 / 7

Product requirements and operating workflows

This specification defines proposed product behavior. It does not imply that every external platform supports every action. Connector-specific capability checks, permissions, commercial access, and vendor documentation govern the available controls. The four releases are Intelligence, Execution, Advertising, and Marketing OS.

Product promise and first customers

The product is a shared marketing workspace for commerce brands and their agencies. It connects merchant facts, observed search visibility, content work, campaign execution, and commercial outcomes. A customer should be able to answer: what is happening, what should we do, who needs to approve it, did it happen, and what changed afterward?

The first customers are Shopify brands and agencies serving those brands. Agencies are customers and delivery partners. The product should make their teams more effective, give clients transparent approvals, and preserve agency expertise through reusable workflows. A brand can eventually contain multiple stores, websites, languages, currencies, and markets. Brand and Shopify store are therefore separate concepts from the first database migration.

Forward-deployed delivery is part of the commercial offer. An implementation team establishes integrations, validates tracking, captures business context, configures permissions, launches the first useful workflow, and transfers operating knowledge. The software remains a shared product. Customer-specific work must be explicitly scoped, estimated, and assessed for reuse.

Personas, ownership, and permissions

Brand owner: owns the workspace, business facts, vendor accounts, billing relationship, agency access, and spending authorization. Marketing lead: prioritizes work, approves content and campaigns within delegated limits, and reviews outcomes. Contributor: produces drafts and briefs but cannot publish or spend by default. Analyst: explores permitted evidence and reports. Client approver: reviews assigned proposals without needing broad operational privileges.

Agency administrator: manages agency staff, client relationships, and reusable agency templates. This role cannot override a brand's restrictions. Agency operator: performs work within the permissions granted by each client. Forward-deployed operator: receives time-bound, purpose-specific support access with an audit trail.

The brand owns business data, vendor accounts, and completed client deliverables. The agency owns its private operating templates and internal notes unless a contract specifies otherwise. Ending an agency relationship should revoke access without deleting brand data or disrupting the customer's access to its own accounts. A verified ownership-transfer procedure is required for exceptional cases.

Shared requirements

  • CORE-001 — Workspace isolation. Every business record belongs to an explicit brand workspace. Acceptance: a user with access to Brand A cannot retrieve Brand B records through UI, API, export, search, storage URL, background job, or report delivery.
  • CORE-002 — Delegated permissions. Reading, drafting, approving, publishing, campaign creation, spending approval, billing, and integrations are separately assignable capabilities. Acceptance: a content editor cannot activate a campaign unless separately granted that capability.
  • CORE-003 — Visible account context. Every consequential confirmation displays brand, external account, market, and destination. Acceptance: switching clients cannot silently submit a draft created under the prior client.
  • CORE-004 — Ownership and offboarding. Brand owners can revoke an agency relationship and export their records. Acceptance: revoked staff lose new access and pending unauthorized writes are blocked; completed brand deliverables remain available.
  • CORE-005 — Evidence and action history. Recommendations reference evidence; writes reference the approved proposal and resulting vendor resource. Acceptance: an operator can reconstruct who proposed, approved, executed, and verified an action.
  • CORE-006 — Subscription and usage controls. The product records included allowances, consumption, overage settings, and billing state. Acceptance: reaching a hard allowance stops new billable work with a clear message and preserves existing data.
  • CORE-007 — Accessible interface. Core workflows support keyboard navigation, clear focus states, readable contrast, descriptive errors, and non-color-only status labels. Acceptance: onboarding, approval, and campaign pause can be completed without a mouse.
  • CORE-008 — Connection health. Every connector displays selected account, granted capabilities, last successful sync, current error, and recovery action. Acceptance: expired authorization is shown as disconnected or degraded rather than as zero business activity.
  • CORE-009 — Human-readable metrics. Metrics disclose time range, timezone, currency, source, freshness, and important definitions. Acceptance: an analyst can explain the denominator behind every visibility percentage.
  • CORE-010 — Support access. Support sessions have an owner, purpose, expiry, and recorded actions. Acceptance: expired support access cannot continue through a background job.

Onboarding and implementation

The signup flow uses email OTP initially. The customer chooses whether they are a brand or agency, creates an organization, names a brand, and establishes an authorized client relationship where applicable. They connect their website or store, choose exact properties and accounts, set reporting timezone and market, enter goals, and identify approvers. The first baseline should be useful even if some connectors are unavailable.

The product imports product facts and assets, then asks the customer to confirm uncertain or commercially sensitive statements. Generated claims are not automatically approved facts. The customer selects competitors, business topics, monitoring frequency, and usage allowance. A progress screen shows actual job states and expected next steps. Onboarding can be resumed without duplicate stores or accounts.

The forward-deployed implementation package produces a connection inventory, a tracking audit, an approved brand-facts register, agreed topics and markets, a baseline report, named approvers, configured spending and publishing boundaries, and a first completed workflow. Any missing tracking or permissions are recorded as known gaps rather than hidden during handover.

  • ONB-001 — Resumable setup. Acceptance: a user leaving after store connection returns to the next unfinished step without reimporting the entire catalogue.
  • ONB-002 — Partial value. Acceptance: a website-only customer can receive a crawl audit while unavailable analytics are explicitly marked unavailable.
  • ONB-003 — Account selection. Acceptance: a Google user with several properties selects the exact property; the product never chooses an arbitrary first property.
  • ONB-004 — Baseline sign-off. Acceptance: the implementation record identifies data gaps, monitoring scope, responsibilities, and customer acknowledgement.

Phase 1: Intelligence

The primary job is to help a marketing team decide what to work on this week. This release includes catalogue inventory, brand knowledge, owned search and analytics views, sampled AI observations, website diagnostics, competitor comparisons, opportunity prioritization, and client reporting.

Brand knowledge contains approved product facts, positioning, audiences, tone, prohibited claims, markets, and business goals. Every material fact has a source, owner, status, and freshness. If the merchant provides margins, shipping constraints, or return policies, the product records their source and avoids treating incomplete figures as audited profitability.

AI observations are organized by question, provider, collection method, market/language setting, timestamp, and sample count. API observations and consumer-interface observations must remain distinguishable. Missing or blocked observations do not count as brand absence. Generated questions are hypotheses, not measured demand. Competitor comparisons use the same eligible observation set and expose coverage differences.

An opportunity contains a plain-English problem, affected pages or products, supporting evidence, proposed action, confidence explanation, effort estimate, business relevance, owner, and status. Ranking is a transparent prioritization aid. It must not invent expected revenue or imply causal certainty.

Requirements and acceptance

  • INT-001 — Catalogue overview. Show imported products, variants, collections, prices, availability, relevant content, and changes. Acceptance: deleted or unavailable products are not silently presented as current inventory.
  • INT-002 — Fact provenance. Store sources and approval status for brand facts. Acceptance: correcting a fact identifies affected drafts or recommendations that may need review.
  • INT-003 — Search and analytics reporting. Display connected property metrics with source definitions. Acceptance: missing dates and reporting delays are visible, and unavailable data is never converted to zero.
  • INT-004 — Observation collection. Support scheduled, collecting, complete, partial, failed, and stale states. Acceptance: a failed run changes coverage rather than lowering the brand's visibility score.
  • INT-005 — Inspectable evidence. Retain permitted raw evidence or reproducible references. Acceptance: clicking a mention or citation reveals the underlying observation, timestamp, and collection method.
  • INT-006 — Comparable competitors. Compare brands only within clearly stated samples. Acceptance: a report cannot compare one competitor's weekly observations with another's daily observations without disclosing the mismatch.
  • INT-007 — Website audit. Findings identify affected URLs, severity rationale, and reproduction evidence. Acceptance: a resolved finding can be rechecked and closed without deleting its history.
  • INT-008 — Opportunity workflow. Support assign, prioritize, comment, snooze, dismiss, reopen, and convert to work. Acceptance: dismissal requires or permits a reason that remains available for future review.
  • INT-009 — Reports. Include methodology, coverage, freshness, selected filters, and annotations. Acceptance: exports preserve the report's date range and denominator rather than changing with a later dashboard refresh.
  • INT-010 — Release readiness. A paying customer connects real data, inspects recommendation evidence, and produces a useful client report. Acceptance: the team demonstrates this with representative brand and agency accounts, including degraded connector states.

Phase 2: Execution

The primary job is turning opportunities into reviewed, live changes. Supported content includes product descriptions, collection pages, buying guides, articles, comparison pages, FAQs, and landing pages. The customer can convert an opportunity to a brief, generate or manually write a draft, review sources, collaborate, approve a specific revision, publish, verify, and examine subsequent outcomes.

The editorial state machine is Idea, Brief, Draft, In review, Changes requested or Approved, Scheduled, Publishing, Published or Failed or Partially published, and Archived. Users see who owns the next step. An approval applies to exact content, destination, and relevant publishing settings. Editing any approved element invalidates that approval.

Remote edits are normal. Before publishing, the system compares the current remote version with the version used to prepare the change. Conflicts require review. The product preserves prior content where possible and documents connector-specific recovery limits. Restoring a page cannot remove all cached, indexed, or previously delivered copies.

Requirements and acceptance

  • EXE-001 — Brief creation. A brief includes audience, purpose, target products, desired action, source facts, and success measures. Acceptance: an operator can trace it to its originating opportunity or create it independently.
  • EXE-002 — Grounded drafting. Drafts distinguish approved facts from unsupported claims. Acceptance: fabricated testimonials, certifications, product specifications, or performance claims are flagged and cannot silently become approved knowledge.
  • EXE-003 — Editor and preview. Provide meaningful before/after diffs, links, assets, and relevant layout previews. Acceptance: an approver can identify exactly what will change without reading unrelated history.
  • EXE-004 — Collaboration. Comments, assignments, requested changes, and resolved discussions persist across versions. Acceptance: comments attached to changed text remain understandable or are marked outdated.
  • EXE-005 — Version-bound approval. Acceptance: changing price language, destination, or content after approval returns the item to review.
  • EXE-006 — Conflict detection. Acceptance: a merchant editing the live page while a draft is pending prevents silent overwrite and offers merge or re-review options.
  • EXE-007 — Reliable publishing. Acceptance: retrying a failed job does not create duplicate pages or assets; partially completed work is visible and recoverable.
  • EXE-008 — Verification and recovery. Acceptance: published content is read back, linked, and compared with the intended version; a supported restoration retrieves the preserved prior version.
  • EXE-009 — Scheduled actions. Acceptance: scheduling shows local timezone and absolute execution time, including daylight-saving transitions.
  • EXE-010 — Outcome review. Acceptance: reports show publication dates and subsequent observations without asserting that every change was caused by the publication.

Phase 3: Advertising

The primary job is launching and improving paid campaigns with control over spending. Integrations expose only platform-supported functionality. Campaign objectives, audiences, bidding, creative formats, product feeds, and reporting vary by provider; a generic interface must not imply identical targeting capabilities.

The workflow begins with account eligibility, permission, billing-readiness, landing-page, and measurement checks. The operator selects products and prepares a campaign brief containing objective, market, schedule, budget, conversion goal, and destination. The system drafts creatives and proposes settings, then presents the exact proposal for approval. Creation should use a paused state where supported, followed by verification and authorized activation.

Campaign state distinguishes draft, awaiting approval, approved, submitting, vendor review, paused, active, delivery limited, rejected, completed, archived, and unknown. Active does not mean delivering. Submitted does not mean reviewed. Vendor state is authoritative for delivery; the product shows its last verified state and timestamp.

Reporting distinguishes spend, clicks, impressions, platform-attributed conversions, observed merchant orders, refunds, and inferred matches. Attribution windows, reporting delays, currencies, and reconciliation rules are visible. Adding platform conversion totals is not equivalent to deduplicating customer orders. Profitability requires adequate merchant cost inputs.

Requirements and acceptance

  • ADS-001 — Account readiness. Acceptance: unavailable write access or account review blocks launch with an actionable explanation while allowing safe draft work.
  • ADS-002 — Platform-specific configuration. Acceptance: unsupported fields cannot be submitted through the shared UI or API.
  • ADS-003 — Exact approval. Approval records account, currency, dates, budget, creative, destination, and relevant settings. Acceptance: changing any consequential field invalidates approval.
  • ADS-004 — Paused creation and verification. Acceptance: a new campaign is verified before activation, and a partially created resource can be resumed without duplication.
  • ADS-005 — Spending authority. Acceptance: users cannot exceed their delegated limits through several small changes or repeated jobs.
  • ADS-006 — External drift. Acceptance: manual changes made in the vendor dashboard are detected before applying a stale proposal.
  • ADS-007 — Confirmed controls. Acceptance: the UI reports paused only after vendor confirmation; ambiguous network outcomes show pending verification rather than success.
  • ADS-008 — Measurement clarity. Acceptance: reports label observed, attributed, modeled, unmatched, and unavailable values appropriately.
  • ADS-009 — Disconnection behavior. Acceptance: revoked credentials block queued writes; the product explains that existing vendor campaigns may continue running.
  • ADS-010 — Launch readiness. An eligible client can approve, create, activate, inspect delivery, and review outcomes for a real campaign with a complete audit trail.

Phase 4: Marketing OS

The primary job is coordinating marketing work and improving it through controlled experiments. This release introduces a shared campaign calendar, cross-channel offers and assets, experiment registry, lifecycle integrations, inventory-aware recommendations, workflow rules, agency templates, and customer APIs.

Automation progresses from observing and notifying to preparing drafts, requesting approval, and executing within narrowly pre-authorized policies. Every workflow has an owner, permitted actions, data dependencies, spending or volume limits, expiry where relevant, and exception handling. AI recommendations do not themselves grant execution authority.

Experiments record a hypothesis, variants, primary metric, duration, stopping rules, eligibility, and known confounders. A before/after chart is not automatically an experiment. Low conversion volume should produce an insufficient-evidence result rather than a fabricated winning variant.

Requirements and acceptance

  • OS-001 — Shared planning. Acceptance: teams can see related content, campaigns, offers, owners, deadlines, and approvals without duplicating the underlying records.
  • OS-002 — Experiment registry. Acceptance: the primary metric and stopping rule are recorded before launch; later changes are visible in history.
  • OS-003 — Workflow builder. Acceptance: only supported connector actions and authorized resources are selectable.
  • OS-004 — Dry runs. Acceptance: a preview run produces proposed actions and explanations without external mutation.
  • OS-005 — Bounded execution. Acceptance: limits apply across repeated runs and concurrent executions, not only to individual actions.
  • OS-006 — Stale-input protection. Acceptance: outdated inventory, revoked permission, or unavailable measurement blocks sensitive actions and alerts the owner.
  • OS-007 — Emergency pause. Acceptance: client-level and global switches stop new actions while preserving in-flight status and audit records.
  • OS-008 — Reusable agency workflows. Acceptance: deploying a template to another client requires explicit resource mapping and cannot reuse the original client's credentials or private data.
  • OS-009 — API and webhooks. Acceptance: customer integrations inherit workspace scopes, rate limits, and action-approval requirements.
  • OS-010 — Reconstructable decisions. Acceptance: a customer can inspect why an automation ran, the evidence used, the rule or approval authorizing it, and the confirmed result.

Billing and operating policies

Charge a recurring software fee primarily around brands or workspaces, with explicit allowances for observations, monitoring frequency, content generation, creative generation, connected accounts, and data retention. Agencies can buy multiple client workspaces without weakening brand isolation. Separate software, implementation services, metered overages, and vendor advertising spend.

Billing states include active, grace period, past due, suspended, canceled, and reactivated; a trial is optional rather than assumed. Suspension should stop new paid compute and unauthorized mutations while preserving permitted read and export access. It should not silently alter live advertising campaigns. Customers must understand which external campaigns continue to spend after software cancellation.

Operational success measures include time to first useful baseline, connector success, sync freshness, accepted opportunities, draft-to-publish time, approval turnaround, verified-action success rate, weekly active brands, retention, support effort, and AI cost per active workspace. Business outcomes include saved operating time and observed commercial trends. Ranking, citations, sales, and return on ad spend are not guaranteed.

Pressure-test conclusions and release discipline

A visibility dashboard alone faces intense competition. Phase one must provide credible evidence and agency reporting, and phase two must follow with useful execution. Supporting all platforms immediately creates false parity and excessive maintenance. Connectors should be capability-based and released individually.

Approval friction can overwhelm teams; batch review should reduce repetitive work while preserving exact resource-level authorization. Agency ownership ambiguity can cause disputes; portability and offboarding need first-release policies. Forward-deployed work can erase margins; implementation packages require defined deliverables, limits, and reuse tracking.

External account edits are normal and must be reconciled. Sparse conversion data should limit automation. Customer data gaps should remain visible. Synthetic questions must not become fabricated search volume. Attribution must not be presented as incrementality.

Build order for Intelligence is tenancy and roles, connectors and health, normalized data, brand facts, observations and audits, evidence views, opportunities, reporting and billing, then onboarding verification. Execution follows with content objects and versions, briefs, editor, approval, publishing, verification, and recovery. Advertising starts with read-only account sync and measurement before enabling drafts, exact approvals, paused creation, activation, and controlled updates. Marketing OS starts with shared events and experiments, then dry-run workflows, bounded execution, lifecycle connectors, and APIs.

The governing release principle is to build the read path, evidence, and recovery path before enabling the write path. Each release must demonstrate its complete customer outcome with realistic failures, not merely a successful interface demonstration.

Technical architecture and implementation specification

This is a proposed engineering design for the complete four-phase product. It uses a modular monolith, one TypeScript repository, a Next.js web application and persistent Node workers on Railway, with Supabase Postgres, Auth and Storage. Each release extends the same product and data model. Implementation must verify pinned dependency compatibility and provider account capabilities before shipping.

1. Deployment topology and repository

Use apps/web for Next.js App Router, dashboard, authentication callbacks, HTTP APIs and fast webhook ingestion. Use apps/worker for integrations, crawling, observation collection, generation, publishing, advertising actions and reporting. Long jobs do not execute inside web requests.

Shared packages are domain for authorization and invariants, db for migrations and typed queries, connectors for provider adapters, ai for structured generation and evaluations, contracts for Zod schemas and job payloads, and ui for shared components. Do not create independent services for each feature. Separate deployment units are justified for web traffic and background workloads, while the domain remains one codebase.

Use separate development, staging and production environments. Production credentials and customer data must not be copied into preview deployments. Keep web, workers and database geographically close. Services remain stateless; original documents, creative and snapshots live in object storage. Container disk is temporary.

Use SQL migrations as the schema source of truth, generated database types for application use, and one typed query layer rather than multiple ORMs. Run schema changes as an explicit deployment step. Use expand-and-contract migrations so an older worker and newer web application can coexist briefly. Every durable job payload has a schema version. Retain handlers for outstanding older jobs during migration.

Postgres contains carefully restricted application tables, private integration tables, analytics tables, an append-only audit schema and a dedicated pg-boss schema. Public API exposure is deliberate; never expose every table merely because it exists. Pin a supported Node LTS version compatible with the chosen Next.js and pg-boss releases.

2. Tenant ownership, agency access and permissions

Separate ownership, access and payment. An organization is a business account. A brand workspace is owned by an organization. An agency organization receives a revocable access grant to one or more brand workspaces. A billing account can pay for those workspaces without becoming the owner of their data.

Core ownership tables are organizations, organization_memberships, brand_workspaces, brand_access_grants, brand_memberships, invitations, billing_accounts, subscriptions and entitlements. Every tenant-owned entity has a non-null brand_id. An agency switcher selects a brand but does not establish authorization by itself.

Owner, administrator, strategist, operator, approver, analyst and billing administrator are convenient role bundles. Domain code checks capabilities such as integrations.connect, content.draft, content.publish, campaign.propose, campaign.approve, campaign.execute and billing.manage. Larger clients can require a different person to approve a proposal from the person who created it.

Integration credentials belong to the brand, not the employee who clicked Connect. Revoking an agency grant must block queued work and new requests immediately. Workers recheck current access before a sensitive action. A brand owner can export data and revoke its agency. Ownership transfer is an explicit audited process with verified destination membership; it is never a side effect of changing the subscription payer.

Supabase row-level security protects user-facing access. Elevated worker credentials can bypass RLS, so workers also require tenant-scoped repositories and explicit authorization. Never use a browser-supplied organization identifier as evidence of access. Never expose elevated database or integration secrets to clients.

3. Data model

Foundation tables include profiles, organizations, memberships, brands, grants, invitations, subscriptions, entitlements, usage reservations, usage ledger, feature flags, notifications and audit events.

Integration tables include connections, credential_versions, connection_capabilities, external_accounts, sync_runs, sync_cursors, webhook_receipts and external_id_mappings. Connections move through pending, active, degraded, reauthorization-required, disabled and revoked states. Record authorized scopes, provider API version, selected account, last success, last error and freshness.

Brand knowledge includes profiles, audiences, product catalogues, products, variants, collections, brand claims, source documents, document versions and optional knowledge chunks. Each factual claim records source, verification state, expiry and approval history. Product price is not enough to infer margin. Store merchant-supplied commercial inputs with their provenance.

Phase-one intelligence includes prompt sets, prompt versions, monitoring configurations, observation runs, answer observations, mentions, citations, competitors, crawl runs, page snapshots, audit findings and opportunities. Every observation records acquisition method, actual surface, provider, known model, locale, timestamp, configured geography, prompt hash, parser version and coverage limitations. API observations and consumer-interface observations are distinct categories.

Phase-two execution includes briefs, content items, immutable content versions, creative assets, publication targets, change sets, approvals, execution runs, publication receipts and restoration snapshots.

Phase-three advertising includes ad accounts, campaigns, ad groups, ads, creative versions, product feeds, campaign proposals, budget policies, provider actions, daily insights, conversion events, orders, refunds and attribution matches. Retain provider-specific configuration alongside normalized common fields.

Phase-four coordination includes marketing plans, calendar items, experiments, variants, automation policies, automation runs, recommendations, agency templates, report definitions and report runs. Do not assume experimentation has established causality merely because a performance dashboard changed.

Use composite tenant references: a content version referencing a content item must reference (brand_id, content_item_id), not only the item UUID. Add unique (brand_id, id) keys where composite foreign keys need them. This prevents accidental cross-brand links at database level.

Keep raw evidence in object storage with content hashes and metadata rather than duplicating large documents in every record.

5. Approval and execution state machine

External mutations follow draft, validated, awaiting approval, approved, queued, executing and terminal states. Terminal states include verified success, failure, unknown outcome and cancellation. An HTTP timeout after submitting a provider action is an unknown outcome until reconciled.

An approval binds the normalized payload hash, version, brand, external account, resource, creative version, destination, budget, schedule, approver identity, capabilities, expiration and provider preconditions. Changing a material field invalidates approval. The UI shows an exact before-and-after preview.

Immediately before execution, the worker checks current permission, connection state, approval expiry, payload hash, budget policy and provider state. If someone changed the campaign directly in Ads Manager, reject or regenerate the stale proposal. After execution, read back the resource and compare the relevant fields. Store a receipt with provider IDs and observed resulting state.

Use provider idempotency keys when supported. Otherwise maintain an action ledger, deterministic external reference and reconciliation strategy. Queue delivery guarantees do not create exactly-once behavior at external APIs. A worker can crash after the provider succeeds but before the local transaction commits.

Restoration is action-specific. A content version can often be restored. An advertisement may be paused or compensated. Money already spent cannot be rolled back. Present the correct operation rather than a universal Undo promise.

6. Internal API contract

Use /api/v1 for application endpoints. Every route derives the user from a verified session, authorizes the supplied brand, validates the body and records sensitive actions. Internal identifiers are UUIDs. Return a stable error shape with code, message, request_id and safe field details. Never include secrets or raw provider responses containing personal data.

Jobs carry brand IDs, entity references, payload version and trace IDs. Secrets are loaded only at execution time. Pagination cursors and chunk checkpoints support recovery. 2. Commercial model and packaging

Sell a recurring subscription per brand workspace with defined usage allowances. Offer an agency agreement with pooled administrative seats and explicit client-level usage allocation. The brand owns its data and connected accounts; the billing payer may be the brand or agency. Losing an agency relationship must not strand the brand's account or require transferring ad-account ownership.

Separate four charges: onboarding/implementation, recurring software, consumption beyond allowance, and optional ongoing managed service. Advertising spend remains billed by the advertising platform to the advertiser. Avoid holding or reselling media funds in the first releases. Taxes and payment-provider eligibility must be reviewed for the selected incorporation and customer markets before selling; this plan does not select a legal entity.

Initial packaging should expose monitored questions, engines, locations, run frequency, content generations, published assets, connected ad accounts, data retention, and seats. Do not advertise unlimited AI or unlimited monitoring. A monitoring unit is a successful provider observation with its associated metadata, not an arbitrary token count hidden from customers. Failed provider requests are not customer-billable unless a clearly disclosed contract says otherwise. Track actual vendor cost independently of customer billing.

Plans unlock released modules. Do not charge for a future module solely because it appears on the roadmap. Publish upgrade and cancellation behavior before accepting payment. Downgrade prevents new over-limit work while preserving accessible historical work during the contracted retention period. It must not silently delete campaigns or stop external ads. Cancellation prompts the owner to choose how external workflows should be handled and records the result.

3. Success metrics and measurement definitions

The primary operational metric is the number of brands completing a verified, approved marketing action and reviewing its outcome during the month. This captures actual use beyond generating drafts. Track revenue retention and contribution margin alongside it. Neither one dashboard visit nor an AI-generated recommendation counts as completed customer value.

Phase 1 metrics: connection completion, data freshness, valid observation coverage, time to first evidence-backed opportunity, weekly active operator, and report usefulness feedback. Phase 2: approved-to-published rate, publish failures, revisions per approved asset, verified restoration success, and post-publication outcome reviews. Phase 3: measurement readiness before launch, rejected creative rate, verified launch success, spend reconciliation, attributable conversions where available, and operator time per campaign. Phase 4: experiment completion, automation exception rate, action reversal rate, time saved, and incremental outcome evidence where experiments support it.

Visibility share means brand mentions divided by valid observations within the specified fixed question set, engine, geography, and period. It is not market share. Citation share uses citations as its denominator and is separately labeled. Failed runs are excluded from the ratio and shown as missing coverage. Changing the tracked question set creates a new comparison cohort rather than rewriting history.

Platform ROAS, when available, is platform-attributed conversion value divided by that platform's spend under its stated window. Store revenue and net revenue after refunds are separate measures. Never sum platform-attributed conversions across platforms into a purported count of unique orders. Currency conversion requires a timestamped rate source and explicit reporting currency. Keep original money amounts in integer minor units or exact decimals with currency; never floating-point arithmetic.

  1. Implement content briefs, source references, immutable versions, editorial assignments, structured brand rules, and generation cost budgets.
  2. Build editor, review comments, diffs, previews, approval expiration, scheduling, and exact-version authorization.

  5. Add landing-page templates, internal link proposals, structured-data validation tasks, and external technical-task handoffs. Customer DNS and theme changes require explicit implementation scope.

Phase 3: approximately 6–10 weeks

  1. Complete ad-platform access proofs started in phase 1. Implement ChatGPT first, then a second channel based on verified customer demand and developer access; do not build three write integrations simultaneously.
  2. Implement account capabilities, campaign hierarchy, native targeting settings, creative uploads, feed validation, paused drafts, policy status, preview, and exact-budget approval.
  3. Install conversion collection appropriate to consent and platform requirements. Add event deduplication, order/refund linkage, event-time and reporting-time reconciliation, and reporting-window labels.

  5. Build campaign reporting, daily cost checks, anomaly flags, creative comparisons, and recommendations. Verify totals against provider dashboards on a documented sample and explain residual differences.
  6. Release each channel only after successful end-to-end operation on an authorized account. Unsupported geography or account access stays explicitly unavailable. Manual import/export can be a labeled operating mode, never disguised as live synchronization.

Phase 4: approximately 6–8 weeks, then iterative expansion

  1. Add shared campaign calendars, experiments, customer-defined goals, cross-channel tasks, and inventory-aware recommendations.
  2. Add Klaviyo and CRM connectors after agreeing which objects and actions are useful. Begin with read-only insight and approved drafts before enabling sends or workflow edits.
  3. Add bounded rules, simulation, dry runs, approval thresholds, maximum spend, cooldowns, and circuit breakers. Show why a rule fired and which evidence it used.
  4. Add agency templates and reporting customization, customer webhooks/API, export, retention controls, and portfolio administration. Preserve tenant boundaries in every template and aggregate.
  5. Conduct operational drills and profitability review. Increase automation only for actions with proven reversibility, reliable measurement, and sufficiently bounded financial impact.

5. Cost model and capacity planning

Infrastructure subscriptions are the smallest and most predictable part of early costs. A starting Supabase Pro subscription and Railway Pro minimum were previously checked at approximately USD25 and USD20 per month respectively, but usage, additional environments, storage, email, observability, and support raise the actual amount. Recheck vendor pricing before purchase. Development credits and free tiers are not production service guarantees.

Use this monitoring equation: brands × questions × engines × locations × runs per day × repetitions × days. Example: 10 brands × 50 questions × 3 engines × 1 location × 1 daily run × 1 repetition × 30 days = 45,000 observations per month. At an illustrative all-in observation cost of USD0.005, that is USD225; at USD0.03, USD1,350. Those unit costs are sensitivity assumptions, not vendor quotes. Three repetitions triple the observation cost. Two locations double it again.

Monthly variable costs also include input/output tokens for drafts, extraction calls, search tool fees, embeddings where needed, image generation, crawling, browser runtime, storage, egress, payment fees, and support time. Record estimated cost before scheduling large work and actual cost after completion. A per-brand hard cap pauses additional optional runs and notifies an owner. It must not silently disable required security, billing reconciliation, or ad-spend monitoring.

Maintain a contribution-margin report per brand: subscription revenue less directly attributable inference, collection, infrastructure allocation, payment fees, and delivery/support labor. Before setting public prices, run realistic workloads for at least a small representative cohort. Forward-deployed onboarding has an hours budget, an agreed completion definition, and an escalation path for custom requests.

Use open-source components where they reduce ongoing effort: Next.js, TypeScript, Postgres, pg-boss, Zod, Playwright, and a standard UI component library. They still incur engineering and compute costs. Avoid adding a separate vector database, Redis, workflow platform, data warehouse, or third-party OAuth broker until a measured need justifies the extra operational surface. A managed connector broker can become useful later; it does not remove platform app reviews or permissions.

6. Release gates and operational ownership

Every release needs a named engineering owner, product owner, and operational responder. Maintain a release checklist with evidence links, not checkboxes based on memory. A feature is complete only when authorization, success, failure, loading, empty, stale, reconnect, and recovery states work.

Proposed service objectives for initial production: 99.5% monthly application availability; API request latency under 1 second at p95 for ordinary cached reads excluding upstream calls; immediate queued acknowledgement for long-running tasks; and daily integrations fresh within the published connector window. These are internal targets, not customer SLAs until measured. Define restore objectives based on purchased backup capability; target restoration within 8 hours and no more than 24 hours of database loss initially, with stronger recovery required before high-volume financial automation. Backups must include object-storage recovery strategy and credentials handling, not just database rows.

Phase 1 gate: isolation tests pass; billing webhooks reconcile; all evidence is traceable; connector outages are visible; a restore drill succeeds. Phase 2 gate: unapproved or modified drafts cannot publish; remote conflicts are detected; supported restoration is verified. Phase 3 gate: spending cannot start without valid authorization; ambiguous execution is reconciled; budget caps and emergency pause are exercised; reporting differences are documented. Phase 4 gate: every automated action has a policy version, dry-run evidence, cooldown, cap, and kill switch; a responder can identify and stop a faulty rule quickly.

Do not treat a passing unit-test suite as proof of these gates. Retain test artifacts, authorized-account readbacks, reconciliation reports, and incident drill results.

PART 7 / 7

Independent pressure test and required resolutions

Review scope: product direction and the delivery/AI-channel specifications. Findings below are design requirements, not claims that the implementation has passed tests. Severity P0 blocks the relevant release; P1 must be resolved before the affected capability ships; P2 is a planned enhancement. A written mitigation is not equivalent to successful validation.

Release-blocking findings

R01 · P0 · Database recovery can replay already executed financial actions

The general phase-one recovery target permits up to 24 hours of database loss. That is insufficient as the only safety mechanism once external advertising actions are enabled: restoring an older database can remove evidence that an external action succeeded while leaving the campaign live at the provider.

Required resolution: after any restore or suspected ledger loss, start all external mutations disabled. Reconcile every affected account and action interval against the provider before re-enabling workers. Preserve stable provider idempotency keys outside transient queue state; keep a separately recoverable append-only action receipt trail. Prefer point-in-time recovery before financial execution, but do not treat PITR alone as proof of consistency. Never auto-replay stale mutation jobs after restore.

Acceptance test: execute a campaign activation, restore a database snapshot predating activation, and show that the recovery process detects the live campaign and does not duplicate creation or activation work. Record the recovered authorization and external-state reconciliation.

R02 · P0 · A local budget cap cannot guarantee provider spend stops instantly

An application cap, queue lock, or polling alert cannot prevent all provider-side spending between syncs, during an outage, or after credentials are revoked. A kill switch can immediately block our new actions; stopping already-running media requires a successful provider action or native platform limit.

Required resolution: distinguish local execution limits, provider-native budget settings, account spend limits, and reporting lag. Use native limits where supported. Show confirmed pause separately from pause requested/unknown. Do not claim zero overspend or instantaneous stop. Define escalation to the account owner when our connector cannot stop a live campaign.

Acceptance test: simulate provider outage during emergency pause. UI must show unresolved external state, prevent further local mutations, expose affected accounts, and provide an operational escalation path.

R03 · P0 · Account ownership and agency creation need an explicit authority model

Brand-owned workspaces are correct, but an agency may create a client workspace before the brand owner joins. A connected account credential proves access, not legal ownership of the brand or unlimited authority to delegate access.

Required resolution: introduce an agency-created, unclaimed workspace state. Require a documented authorization attestation, verified brand-owner invitation/claim process, and explicit distinction between agency payer and brand owner. Disputes go through an audited support process; no automated ownership transfer based solely on email-domain matching. A pending ownership claim must not silently transfer credentials.

Acceptance test: two agencies claim the same brand, and a third-party consultant holds ad-account access. The system must preserve existing authority, flag conflicting claims, and avoid automatic data disclosure or account transfer.

R04 · P0 · Runtime authorization must survive revocation and concurrent changes

Checking approval when a job is queued is not enough. Agency membership, brand permissions, approval revision, account readiness, and remote resource state can change before execution.

Required resolution: check authorization immediately before the external call, bind it to the exact immutable payload/account/resource, and use bounded leases or locking for concurrent execution. For long multi-step launches, recheck between consequential steps. Define what happens when an approver loses authority after approval: default to invalidating pending execution unless the brand's explicit policy says otherwise.

Acceptance test: revoke the agency grant between creative upload and activation. No further external mutation may occur, and already-created paused resources remain visible for owner recovery.

High-priority gaps

R05 · P1 · Four phase estimates and promised scope need reconciliation

The current per-phase ranges total 24–36 weeks, which is arithmetically consistent. However, three engineers delivering multiple integration reviews, a mature editorial experience, attribution, lifecycle execution, and agency operations in that period is a planning hypothesis. Connector review timelines and customer-specific installation can dominate the critical path.

Resolution: use release gates, not dates, as commitment criteria. Limit each phase to named first connectors, then list independently released expansion connectors. Track integration access, engineering, UX, security review, and customer validation as separate workstreams. Reserve explicit capacity for support and external API changes. Re-estimate after the first fully implemented connector and a real onboarding.

R06 · P1 · Phase-one primary metric should not depend on later publishing

The primary metric refers to a verified approved marketing action and outcome review. Phase one is mainly intelligence and does not execute publication or campaigns.

Resolution: define phase-one activation as a verified connection, reviewed baseline, and accepted/assigned evidence-backed opportunity. The broader completed-action metric becomes primary after phase two. Avoid inventing off-platform completion evidence; allow customer-reported completion with that label.

R07 · P1 · Monitoring geography can be configured without being delivered

A locale string in a prompt is not a geographically representative consumer session. Licensed observations may also have imperfect location or account controls.

Resolution: store requested geography and verified collection geography separately. Show unsupported geography as unavailable; do not infer location fidelity from prompt wording. Keep API, consumer-interface, and owned-analytics cohorts separate. Model/version changes and parser revisions need comparison annotations.

Acceptance test: a provider ignores geographic settings or does not expose location. The observation remains usable for its actual collection mode but cannot be labeled a verified local consumer result.

R08 · P1 · Statistical confidence and low sample sizes require defined behavior

Visibility changes, creative comparisons, and automated optimization can amplify noise. A dashboard with one observation per question does not justify strong causal or statistical conclusions.

Resolution: show sample counts and missingness, distinguish descriptive changes from tested effects, and define minimum evidence requirements per recommendation. Experiment registration must include primary metric, allocation unit, stopping rules, and contamination risks. Suppress confident winner labels when evidence is insufficient. Do not bolt generic significance scores onto non-independent AI observations.

R09 · P1 · Attribution identity and consent are incomplete without an event contract

Stable IDs prevent duplicate transport, but they do not automatically establish user consent, cross-device identity, or a valid connection between an ad click and order.

Resolution: specify event name, schema version, event time, source, order ID, consent state at collection and dispatch, provider destination, identifier normalization, and retention. Do not infer permission for marketing emails from a purchase. Limit customer identifiers to the specific approved purpose. Unmatched orders remain unmatched. Record refunds separately from platform conversion revisions.

Acceptance test: consent is absent or withdrawn before dispatch, an order is refunded, and a duplicate browser/server event arrives. The system respects dispatch policy, deduplicates logical events, and updates commerce outcomes without rewriting historical provider attribution.

R10 · P1 · AI factual checks need a measurable evaluation gate

Grounded generation and editorial approval are good requirements, but a model checking another model is not sufficient proof that claims are correct.

Resolution: use deterministic checks for prices, currencies, availability, numeric specifications, required disclosures, and prohibited claims where possible. Maintain a reviewed fixture set spanning easy, ambiguous, adversarial, and stale inputs. Define release thresholds before evaluation, preserve failures, and prevent model upgrades from silently changing production behavior. Unknown claims must remain flagged rather than being converted to approved facts by the generation pipeline.

R11 · P1 · Credential lifecycle and support access need operational detail

Encrypted tokens alone do not solve compromised support access, refresh races, lost keys, or accidental exposure in error tracking.

Resolution: specify key rotation, reconnect behavior, token-revocation response, secret redaction, privileged access approval, support-session expiry, audit retention, and incident owner. Add OTP abuse limits and invitation expiry. Ad-spend authorization should support step-up authentication for sensitive actions even if ordinary login is email OTP.

Acceptance test: stolen session, replayed OAuth state, expired invitation, concurrent refresh, and revoked credential all fail safely without cross-client leakage.

R12 · P1 · Billing must not interrupt safety operations

Suspension and cost limits should prevent optional new work, but stopping reconciliation, external-state visibility, or incident notifications could leave live campaigns unmanaged.

Resolution: identify essential operational jobs that continue during billing grace/suspension, with bounded internal cost. Provide emergency controls and export access according to contract. A subscription cancellation is not an advertising cancellation. Make that distinction explicit during offboarding and preserve the customer's selection.

R13 · P1 · Content restoration and landing-page hosting are distinct products

Publishing to Shopify/WordPress, restoring prior content, and hosting new landing pages involve different permissions and operational responsibilities. Themes, plugin-generated HTML, media, redirects, and SEO metadata may not restore as one atomic unit.

Resolution: define a connector capability matrix for create/update/delete/restore/preview and a separate landing-page deployment strategy. Start with customer-platform templates where feasible. Specify domain ownership, redirects, asset retention, preview access, canonical URLs, and broken-link checks. Use compensation and manual recovery where true rollback is impossible.

R14 · P1 · Data deletion must include derived artifacts and backups policy

Deleting a source document alone does not remove embeddings, drafts, reports, snapshots, cached prompts, or third-party processor copies.

Resolution: maintain lineage from source to derived objects; define deletion/export scope, processor deletion mechanisms, retention exceptions, backup expiry, and tombstones preventing re-import. Legal review sets required retention rather than product code assuming a universal rule. Cancellation and privacy deletion are separate operations.

R15 · P1 · Full workflow builder conflicts with a bounded early phase four

A general visual workflow editor and an agent framework can expand scope beyond the estimated phase-four window.

Resolution: ship a finite library of parameterized workflows first: stale-stock alert, approval reminder, low-spend diagnostic, weekly client report, and approved campaign change. Treat arbitrary branching, custom code, marketplace actions, and user-defined agents as separately scoped future enhancements. Every template has tested action bounds and failure behavior.

Overall verdict

R16 · P1 · Conversion uniqueness must not split one event by transport

The architecture proposes uniqueness on (brand_id, source, event_id) while also requiring browser/server deduplication. If source means browser versus server, the same event is stored twice. Clarify source as the business system of record, not transport. Use a canonical business-event key such as (brand_id, event_type, canonical_event_id) and separate delivery records keyed by destination account and transport. Preserve both incoming receipts without counting two conversions. For orders, include store identity in the external-order uniqueness key because native IDs may collide across stores.

Acceptance test: identical purchase arrives once from browser and twice from server for the same store/order; canonical purchase count is one, while all three receipts remain auditable.

The four-release direction is coherent if each release remains a complete workflow and external capabilities are gated individually. The architecture should prioritize tenant isolation, evidence provenance, exact approval, and recoverable execution before adding breadth. The largest unresolved commercial question is whether the combined workflow saves enough agency/operator time to justify recurring fees and forward-deployed delivery. Validate this with paid customers and measured operational effort, while keeping the larger product roadmap intact.
